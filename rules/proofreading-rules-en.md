# Proofreading Rules (English)

> Based on The Chicago Manual of Style, 18th Edition. Organized for automated proofreading.

## 1. Spelling & Word Confusions

### 1.1 Commonly Confused Words (from CMOS Ch.5 §5.254)

- accept / except — "accept" (to receive) ≠ "except" (to exclude)
- adverse / averse — "adverse" (unfavorable, of things) ≠ "averse" (opposed, of people)
- affect / effect — "affect" (vb., to influence) ≠ "effect" (n., result); "effect" as vb. = "to bring about"
- allude / elude — "allude" (hint indirectly) ≠ "elude" (avoid capture)
- already / all ready — "already" (by this time) ≠ "all ready" (fully prepared)
- altogether / all together — "altogether" (entirely) ≠ "all together" (in unison)
- amount / number — "amount" for mass nouns ≠ "number" for count nouns
- assure / ensure / insure — "assure" (reassure a person) ≠ "ensure" (make certain) ≠ "insure" (underwrite)
- a / an — "a" before consonant sounds, "an" before vowel sounds: "an hour" ≠ "a hour"
- between / among — "between" (one-to-one relationships) ≠ "among" (collective)
- biannual / biennial — "biannual" (twice a year) ≠ "biennial" (every two years)
- blatant / flagrant — "blatant" (obviously bad) ≠ "flagrant" (brazenly illegal)
- born / borne — "born" (given birth) ≠ "borne" (carried, past part. of bear)
- bring / take — "bring" (toward speaker) ≠ "take" (away from speaker)
- capital / capitol — "capital" (city) ≠ "capitol" (building)
- censor / censure — "censor" (suppress) ≠ "censure" (strongly criticize)
- chord / cord — "chord" (musical notes) ≠ "cord" (rope, string)
- cite / site / sight — "cite" (reference) ≠ "site" (location) ≠ "sight" (vision)
- compliment / complement — "compliment" (praise) ≠ "complement" (completes)
- comprise / compose — "comprise" (whole includes parts) ≠ "compose" (parts make up whole)
- continual / continuous — "continual" (recurring, with breaks) ≠ "continuous" (unbroken)
- "couldn't care less" — standard; "could care less" is illogical
- deserts / desserts — "deserts" (what one deserves) ≠ "desserts" (sweets)
- discreet / discrete — "discreet" (tactful) ≠ "discrete" (separate, distinct)
- disinterested / uninterested — "disinterested" (impartial) ≠ "uninterested" (bored)
- elicit / illicit — "elicit" (draw out) ≠ "illicit" (unlawful)
- emigrate / immigrate — "emigrate" (leave a country) ≠ "immigrate" (enter a country)
- eminent / imminent — "eminent" (distinguished) ≠ "imminent" (about to happen)
- explicit / implicit — "explicit" (clearly stated) ≠ "implicit" (implied)
- farther / further — "farther" (physical distance) ≠ "further" (figurative degree)
- faze / phase — "faze" (disturb) ≠ "phase" (stage)
- flaunt / flout — "flaunt" (show off) ≠ "flout" (openly disobey)
- flounder / founder — "flounder" (struggle) ≠ "founder" (sink, fail)
- hanged / hung — "hanged" (execution by rope) ≠ "hung" (suspended)
- historic / historical — "historic" (momentous) ≠ "historical" (pertaining to history)
- hoard / horde — "hoard" (stash) ≠ "horde" (large crowd)
- "home in" — not "hone in" (home = target; hone = sharpen)
- if / whether — "if" (conditional) ≠ "whether" (introduces alternative)
- imply / infer — "imply" (speaker suggests) ≠ "infer" (listener deduces)
- "irregardless" — nonstandard; use "regardless"
- lay / lie — "lay" (transitive, put down) ≠ "lie" (intransitive, recline)
- led / lead — "led" is past tense of "lead"; often misspelled "lead"
- less / fewer — "less" (mass nouns) ≠ "fewer" (count nouns)
- loathe / loath — "loathe" (vb., detest) ≠ "loath" (adj., reluctant)
- loose / lose — "loose" (not tight) ≠ "lose" (misplace)
- nauseous / nauseated — "nauseous" (causing nausea) ≠ "nauseated" (feeling sick)
- past / passed — "past" (adj./n./prep.) ≠ "passed" (vb., past tense of pass)
- peak / peek / pique — "peak" (summit) ≠ "peek" (glance) ≠ "pique" (annoyance)
- precede / proceed — "precede" (go before) ≠ "proceed" (go forward)
- principal / principle — "principal" (main, person) ≠ "principle" (rule)
- proscribe / prescribe — "proscribe" (prohibit) ≠ "prescribe" (order)
- raise / raze — "raise" (lift up) ≠ "raze" (demolish)
- reign / rein — "reign" (rule) ≠ "rein" (strap); "free rein" ≠ "free reign"
- reluctant / reticent — "reluctant" (unwilling to act) ≠ "reticent" (unwilling to speak)
- role / roll — "role" (function, part) ≠ "roll" (rotate, list, bread)
- stationary / stationery — "stationary" (not moving) ≠ "stationery" (writing paper)
- strait / straight — "strait" (narrow channel) ≠ "straight" (not bent)
- that / which — "that" (restrictive, no comma) ≠ "which" (nonrestrictive, comma)
- their / there / they're — "their" (possessive) ≠ "there" (place) ≠ "they're" (they are)
- waive / wave — "waive" (relinquish) ≠ "wave" (move to and fro)
- wreak / reek — "wreak" (cause havoc) ≠ "reek" (stink)
- your / you're — "your" (possessive) ≠ "you're" (you are)

## 2. Grammar (from CMOS Ch.5)

### 2.1 Subject-Verb Agreement

- Compound subjects joined by "and" take a plural verb. "Tom and Jerry are" ≠ "Tom and Jerry is"
- With "either/or" or "neither/nor", the verb agrees with the nearer subject. "Neither the twins nor Jon is prepared" ≠ "Neither the twins nor Jon are prepared"
- When pronouns are joined by "or", "either/or", or "neither/nor", the verb agrees with the nearer pronoun. "Either he or I am in for a surprise" ≠ "Either he or I is in for a surprise"
- Collective nouns conveying unity take a singular verb. "The nation is powerful" ≠ "The nation are powerful"
- Collective nouns conveying plurality take a plural verb. "The faculty were divided" ≠ "The faculty was divided" (when meaning individual members)
- "Majority" takes singular verb as a collective noun, but plural when followed by plural "of"-phrase. "The majority is wheat" ≠ "The majority are wheat"; but "The majority of animals never attack" ≠ "The majority of animals never attacks"
- Intervening phrases like "as well as", "along with", "together with" do NOT make a singular subject plural. "The bride as well as her bridesmaids was dressed" ≠ "The bride as well as her bridesmaids were dressed"
- "One of those who/that" takes a plural verb — the antecedent is the plural noun, not "one". "She is one of those employees who work tirelessly" ≠ "She is one of those employees who works tirelessly"
- False attraction to predicate noun: verb agrees with subject, not the predicate noun. "My downfall is sweets" ≠ "My downfall are sweets"; "Sweets are my downfall" ≠ "Sweets is my downfall"
- Indefinite pronouns (anybody, anyone, everybody, everyone, nobody, somebody) take singular verbs. "Everyone is enjoying" ≠ "Everyone are enjoying"
- "A number of" takes plural verb; "the number of" takes singular verb. "A number of people have changed" ≠ "A number of people has changed"; "The number of options keeps changing" ≠ "The number of options keep changing"
- Corporate names plural in form take singular verbs in American English. "General Motors reports" ≠ "General Motors report"
- "Percentage" + "of" + plural noun takes plural verb. "A small percentage of seeds are unlikely" ≠ "A small percentage of seeds is unlikely"
- "Each" and "every" as subjects take singular verbs. "Each student brings" ≠ "Each student bring"
- Amounts and measurements (sums of money, periods of time) take singular verbs when treated as a unit. "Five dollars is too much" ≠ "Five dollars are too much"

### 2.2 Pronoun Agreement

- A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in gender, person, and number.
- Singular "they" is now standard for a person of unspecified gender. "Somebody forgot their coat" (acceptable; takes plural verb: "they have" ≠ "they has")
- In "one of those who/that", the relative pronoun takes its number from the plural antecedent. "One of those people who care" ≠ "One of those people who cares"
- A relative pronoun's antecedent should immediately precede it for clarity. "The diadem that I told you about" (clear) ≠ "The bedroom of the villa, which was painted pink" (ambiguous)
- "Who" is nominative (subject); "whom" is objective (object of verb/prep). "Who should I say is calling?" ≠ "Whom should I say is calling?" (who is subject of "is")
- The pronoun's function within ITS OWN clause determines case, not the main clause. "I'll talk to whoever will listen" ≠ "I'll talk to whomever will listen" (whoever is subject of "will listen")
- After "than" or "as...as", pronoun case depends on the understood verb. "You are wiser than I [am]" (formal) ≠ "You are wiser than me" (informal)
- After a preposition, use the objective case. "Between you and me" ≠ "Between you and I"
- Pronoun after a linking verb ("be") should be nominative. "It was she who asked" ≠ "It was her who asked" (formal)
- Personal pronoun joined by "and"/"or" must match the case of its coordinate. "He and she are colleagues" ≠ "Him and she are colleagues"
- Possessive pronouns (its, your, their, whose) take NO apostrophe. "Its" (possessive) ≠ "It's" (contraction of "it is"); "Whose book" ≠ "Who's book"
- When a gerund is modified by a noun/pronoun, use the possessive form. "My painting your fence depends on your paying" ≠ "Me painting your fence depends on you paying"
- "Every" + singular noun + singular pronoun. "Every student should bring his or her (or their) book" — singular "their" now accepted

### 2.3 Verb Tense & Mood

- Subjunctive mood uses "were" for contrary-to-fact statements, regardless of subject number. "If I were you" ≠ "If I was you"; "If she were any different" ≠ "If she was any different"
- Subjunctive mood is required after verbs of demand, suggestion, or recommendation. "The landlord insists that the dog go" ≠ "The landlord insists that the dog goes"; "I recommend that she take a vacation" ≠ "I recommend that she takes a vacation"
- Use "if I had gone", NOT "if I would have gone" in conditional past. "If I had gone, I would have seen her" ≠ "If I would have gone, I would have seen her"
- "If" + indicative for open conditions (possibly true). "If I am right, please call" ≠ "If I be right, please call"
- Sequence of tenses: past-perfect ("had done") for action completed before another past action. "The rain had begun falling before we arrived" ≠ "The rain began falling before we arrived" (ambiguous sequence)
- "Shall" in American English is limited to first-person questions and legal requirements. "Shall we go?" (OK); "The debtor shall pay within 30 days" (legal OK); elsewhere use "will"
- Present tense for timeless facts and literary descriptions. "Julius Caesar describes his strategies" ≠ "Julius Caesar described his strategies" (when referring to extant work)
- Progressive tense showing ongoing action: use "be" + present participle. "He is playing tennis" (progressive) ≠ "He plays tennis" (simple present, habitual)
- Do NOT use "shall" where "will" is expected in everyday American English. "I will go" ≠ "I shall go" (marked as British/formal)
- "Can" denotes ability/permission; "may" denotes permission or possibility. "May I go to the movies?" (permission) ≠ "Can I go to the movies?" (colloquial for permission, but acceptable informally)
- "Might" expresses stronger doubt than "may". "We might get there on time (if traffic clears)" vs "We may get there on time" (more likely)
- "Must not" is more intensive than "may not" for prohibition. "You must not climb that tree" (stronger) ≠ "You may not climb that tree" (less emphatic)
- Avoid double modals ("might could", "used to could") in Standard English

### 2.4 Modifiers

- "Only" should be placed immediately before the word it modifies. "I bought only tomatoes at the market" (bought nothing else) ≠ "I only bought tomatoes at the market" (ambiguous in writing)
- Dangling participle: the subject of the main clause must logically perform the participle's action. "To repair your car properly, you must take it to a mechanic" ≠ "To repair your car properly, it must be sent to a mechanic"
- Dangling gerund: clarify who is performing the gerund's action. "After Gero finished the research, the screenplay was easy" ≠ "After finishing the research, the screenplay was easy" (who finished?)
- Dangling participle with passive voice: use active voice to give the participle a logical subject. "Finding that the questions were not ambiguous, the teacher did not change the exam grades" ≠ "Finding that the questions were not ambiguous, the exam grades were not changed"
- Avoid "it" or "there" as subject after a participial phrase — creates dangling modifier. "Our review shows that no consensus exists" ≠ "Reviewing the suggestions, it is clear that no consensus exists"
- Place adverbs as close as possible to the word they modify. "We nearly lost all our camping equipment" (saved it) vs "We lost nearly all our camping equipment" (almost everything lost) — different meanings
- Adverb within verb phrase: place after first auxiliary. "The administration has consistently repudiated this view" — split is fine
- Split infinitive is acceptable when it adds emphasis or natural sound. "To boldly go" is fine — no rule against splitting infinitives
- Coordinate adjectives in a series should be separated by commas. "Skilled, experienced chess player" ≠ "Skilled experienced chess player" (if "and" fits between them, use comma)
- Noncoordinate adjectives (where one modifies the noun-plus-modifier) take no comma. "White brick house" ≠ "White, brick house" (brick house, white one)
- Do NOT use double comparatives or double superlatives. "More greener" ≠ "More green" or "greener"; "Most unkindest" ≠ "Most unkind"
- Noncomparable adjectives (unique, impossible, entire, pregnant) should not take comparative/superlative or intensifiers. "More unique" ≠ "Unique"; "Very impossible" ≠ "Impossible"
- Flat adverbs (drive slow, hold tight) are acceptable — not all adverbs need "-ly"
- Adverbs do NOT follow linking verbs; use predicate adjectives instead. "I feel bad" ≠ "I feel badly" (badly = impaired sense of touch)
- "Good" is an adjective; "well" is an adverb (except when meaning "healthy"). "She did well" ≠ "She did good"; "I feel well" (healthy) is OK
- A misplaced prepositional phrase can create ambiguity. "Is there a person with a small dog named Sandy here?" (ambiguous: person or dog named Sandy?) — rephrase for clarity

### 2.5 Parallel Structure

- Items in a parallel series must share the same grammatical form (all nouns, all verbs, all phrases). "She did volunteer work in the community kitchen, the homeless shelter, and her church" ≠ "She did volunteer work in the community kitchen, the homeless shelter, and taught free ESL classes"
- Correlative conjunctions (either/or, neither/nor, both/and, not only/but also) must frame grammatically identical elements. "I would like either to go into business or to write articles" ≠ "I would like either to go into business or else to write articles"
- "Not only"/"but also" placement: the verb should apply to both elements. "Our guests ate not only all the turkey but both pumpkin pies" ≠ "Our guests not only ate all the turkey but both pumpkin pies as well"
- In a parallel series of prepositional phrases, repeat the preposition unless all use the same one. "I looked in the sock drawer, in the laundry hamper, in the bathroom, and under the bed" ≠ "I looked for my keys in the sock drawer, the laundry hamper, the bathroom, and under the bed"
- If an auxiliary verb precedes a series of verb phrases, it must apply to all. "The proposal would streamline the process, speed up decisions, and save money" ≠ "The proposal would streamline the process, speed up decisions, and has proved to save money"
- Keep the same voice (active/passive) across parallel elements. "He came, he saw, he conquered" (parallel active) ≠ "He came, he saw, and the city was conquered by him" (mixed voice)
- Keep the same person and number across parallel elements. "Every student must bring their book and complete the assignment" ≠ "Every student must bring their book and you must complete the assignment"

### 2.6 Prepositions

- Ending a sentence with a preposition is acceptable. "This is the case I told you about" is fine ≠ "This is the case about which I told you" (stilted; not wrong but avoid overcorrection)
- Use objective case after a preposition. "Between you and me" ≠ "Between you and I"; "With him and her" ≠ "With he and she"
- "Different from" is preferred over "different than" (though "different than" is acceptable before a clause). "This is different from that" ≠ "This is different than that"
- "Compare with" for literal comparison; "compare to" for metaphorical. "Compare this with that" (literal) ≠ "Compare this to that" (figurative/poetic)
- "Comply with" (NOT "comply to"). "Comply with the rules" ≠ "Comply to the rules"
- "Center on" (NOT "center around"). "Center on the main issue" ≠ "Center around the main issue"
- "Based on" (NOT "based off of"). "Based on the evidence" ≠ "Based off of the evidence"
- "Regardless" (NOT "irregardless"). "Regardless of the outcome" ≠ "Irregardless of the outcome"
- Verb + preposition idioms: "fill A with B" ≠ "fill A into B"; "substitute B for A" ≠ "substitute A with B"; "instill B in/into A" ≠ "instill A with B"
- Avoid clashing prepositions (same word back-to-back). "He gives in in every argument" — recast to "In every argument, he gives in"
- Use single-word prepositions instead of verbose phrasal ones. "About" ≠ "With regard to" / "In connection with" (prefer simpler form)
- Preposition "like" as conjunction is considered nonstandard in formal prose; use "as" or "as if". "He ran as if he were scared" ≠ "He ran like he was really scared" (formal)
- "Of" after "off" is redundant. "Step off the platform" ≠ "Step off of the platform"
- "Outside" (NOT "outside of") in spatial sense. "Outside the house" ≠ "Outside of the house"

### 2.7 Conjunctions

- "That" introduces restrictive (essential) clauses; "which" introduces nonrestrictive (nonessential) clauses. "The book that I borrowed is overdue" (essential) ≠ "The book, which I borrowed, is overdue" (nonessential — different meaning)
- Restrictive clauses take NO comma; nonrestrictive clauses take commas. "The students who studied passed" (restrictive) ≠ "The students, who studied, passed" (nonrestrictive — all students studied)
- "That" referring to a person is acceptable, though "who" is preferred. "The woman who spoke" ≠ "The woman that spoke" (who preferred for persons)
- Omitting "that" after certain verbs may cause ambiguity. "The plaintiff charged that the expenses were recorded" ≠ "The plaintiff charged the expenses were recorded" (ambiguous: charged the expenses or stated?)
- "And" is a coordinating conjunction joining equals. "He and she" ≠ "Him and she"
- Correlative conjunctions (as/as, so/as, if/then) must pair correctly. "As tall as" ≠ "As tall than"; "Not only...but also" ≠ "Not only...but as well"
- Beginning a sentence with "and", "but", or "so" is grammatically correct (common in first-rate writing)
- "However" can begin a sentence but is more effective mid-sentence for emphasis. "Soon, however, it turned out to be dull" (better) ≠ "However, it soon turned out to be dull" (fine but heavier)
- "But" as sentence starter requires true contrast with preceding idea. Don't use "but" where "and" would work
- "Because" introduces reason/cause; "since" can also introduce time. "Because it was raining, we stayed" (reason) — flag if "since" is ambiguous for time vs reason
- "Whether" (not "if") introduces indirect questions and alternatives. "He asked whether we were going" (formal) ≠ "He asked if we were going" (informal)
- "Than" functions as a conjunction (not preposition) in formal prose: "You are taller than I [am]" ≠ "You are taller than me" (informal)

### 2.8 Syntax

- False attraction: intervening prepositional phrases do not change subject number. "The man with two daughters has arrived" ≠ "The man with two daughters have arrived"
- Unclear antecedent: a pronoun should refer to ONE unambiguous noun. "After the news came out, the CEO fired the aide, who was the target" — rephrase if unclear who "who" refers to
- Dangling participle with "it" or "there" expletive: the participle needs a logical subject. "Our review of the suggestions shows that no consensus exists" ≠ "Reviewing the suggestions, it is clear that no consensus exists"
- Dangling participle — a participle at the beginning of a sentence must modify the grammatical subject. "Shaming, often used in early America, is an effective punishment" ≠ "Frequently used in early America, experts suggest that shaming is an effective punishment"
- Avoid multiple "it" references in close proximity (pronoun vs. expletive). Rephrase to avoid confusion
- "Any-" words (anyone, anything, anywhere) must follow "not" — NOT "some-" words. "I don't want to see anybody" ≠ "I don't want to see somebody" (unless meaning a specific unnamed person)
- Double negatives in Standard English cancel each other out. "He didn't not say anything" = "He did say something" (mild positive)
- Parallel structure in lists: all items must share the same grammatical function. "The candidate is a former county judge, state senator, and two-term attorney general" ≠ "The candidate is a former county judge, state senator, and served two terms as attorney general"
- Subject-verb inversion after negative adverbs: "scarcely had we arrived" ≠ "scarcely we had arrived"
- "Because" clauses should not be used as subjects without restructuring. "Just because X doesn't mean Y" — acceptable informally, but recast in formal prose: "The fact that X does not mean that Y"
- Avoid ambiguous negation: "I wouldn't be surprised if Dan doesn't find the hammer" — unclear whether Dan will find it or not
- Use "none" instead of "not any" for more concise negation. "We saw no one" ≠ "We did not see anyone" (both correct; shorter preferred)
- Appositive clauses vs. relative clauses: "that" in an appositive can stand alone. "The report that the broadcaster had died" (self-contained clause) ≠ restrictive relative clause
- Conditional clauses: "if" + past perfect ("had done") for unreal past conditions. "If he had remembered his raincoat" ≠ "If he would have remembered his raincoat"

## 3. Punctuation (from CMOS Ch.6)

### 3.1 Commas

**Serial / Oxford Comma**
- Use serial comma before "and" or "or" in a series of three or more. "a, b, and c" (Chicago required).
- Omit serial comma before ampersand (&). "Winken, Blinken & Nod"
- "As well as" cannot substitute for "and" in a series. "Mazda, two Corvettes, and three Bugattis, as well as a battered Plymouth Belvedere" (comma before "as well as" keeps series straight).

**Independent Clauses**
- Comma before coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, so, yet) joining independent clauses. "We activated the alarm, but the intruder was already inside."
- Omit comma for very short clauses: "Electra played the guitar and Tambora sang."
- No comma normally before conjunction joining compound predicate (shared subject). "He printed out a week's worth of crossword puzzles and arranged them on his clipboard."
- Comma splice (joining independent clauses with only a comma) is an error in formal prose. Fix by adding conjunction or switching to semicolon/period.

**Dependent Clauses**
- Introductory dependent clause: followed by comma. "If you accept our conditions, we will agree to the proposal."
- Restrictive dependent clause after main clause: no comma. "We will agree to the proposal if you accept our conditions."
- Nonrestrictive dependent clause after main clause: preceded by comma. "I'd like the tom yum, if you don't mind."

**Relative Clauses (that vs. which)**
- Restrictive clause (essential): use "that" (or who/whom/whose), no commas. "The manuscript that the editors submitted was well formatted."
- Nonrestrictive clause (parenthetical): use "which" (or who/whom/whose), set off by commas. "The final manuscript, which was well formatted, was submitted on time."

**Appositives and Descriptive Phrases**
- Nonrestrictive appositive: set off by commas. "The title of Miranda's first musical, In the Heights, refers to..."
- Restrictive appositive (essential to identify the noun): no commas. "The movie version of Miranda's Broadway musical Hamilton is valuable..."
- Spousal/relationship commas: name set off by commas when the person is the only one of that kind. "Desi's wife, Shondra, has a PhD." | "Dillon's dog Spot is a Dalmatian." (multiple dogs, no commas)

**Introductory Words and Phrases**
- Introductory participial phrase: set off by comma. "Exhilarated by her morning workout, she headed for the ocean."
- Introductory adverbial phrase: comma optional, use if misreading likely. "On the other hand, his vices could be considered virtues." | "In 1931 Henrietta turned fifty."
- Yes, no, OK, well: comma follows when introductory. "Yes, it is true." | "No, neither scenario improved."
- Oh/ah: comma follows. "Oh, you're right!"

**Coordinate Adjectives**
- Coordinate adjectives (could be joined by "and"): separate by commas. "a faithful, sincere friend"
- Non-coordinate adjectives (form unit with noun): no commas. "an inexpensive quartz watch" | "traditional religious affiliations"
- Repeated adjective: comma between. "Many, many people have enjoyed the book."

**Dates and Addresses**
- Month-day-year: comma after day and after year (in pairs). "February 2, 2006, at the State Theatre"
- Day-month-year (British): no commas needed. "6 October 2022"
- Month and year only: no comma. "April 2027"
- Named day + year: comma optional. "Thanksgiving Day 1998"
- Address elements in text: set off by commas. "Waukegan, Illinois, is not far from the border."

**Quotations and Questions**
- Direct quotation introduced by said/replied/asked: preceded by comma. "She replied, 'I hope you aren't referring to us.'"
- Quotation introduced by that/whether/if: no comma. "He wondered whether 'to think is to live.'"
- Direct question within sentence: introduced by comma, begins with capital. "She wondered, What am I doing?"
- Indirect question: no comma, no question mark. "She wondered what she was doing."

**Other Comma Rules**
- "Not" phrases: set off by commas. "We hoped the mayor herself, not her assistant, would attend."
- Parenthetical elements: set off by commas (for slight break). "All the test participants, in spite of our initial fears, recovered."
- Conjunctive adverbs (however, therefore, indeed): usually set off by commas. "A truly efficient engine remains, however, a pipe dream."
- "Such as" / "including": nonrestrictive phrases get commas; restrictive phrases do not.
- "Too" / "either" (meaning "also"): comma generally not needed. "I had my cake and ate it too."
- Vocative comma: name/word in direct address set off by comma(s). "Ms. Jones, please come in." | "Kiss me, you fool!"
- Elision comma: indicates omitted word. "In Illinois there are seventeen such schools; in Ohio, twenty; in Indiana, thirteen."
- Jr., Sr., II, III, Inc., Ltd.: commas not required. "John Doe Sr." | "Jason Deer III" | "QuartzMove Inc."
- Comma never precedes a closing parenthesis; it follows the closing parenthesis.

### 3.2 Semicolons

- Between independent clauses not joined by conjunction (closer than period). "She spent much of her time immersed in the ocean; no mere water-resistant watch would do."
- Before conjunctive adverbs (however, therefore, thus, hence, indeed, accordingly, besides) joining independent clauses. "The accuracy was never in question; besides, he was an expert."
- Semicolon before coordinating conjunction in formal prose when clauses have internal punctuation or for stronger separation.
- In complex series with internal commas: use semicolons between items. "France, 4; Germany, 5; Great Britain, 1; Italy, 3; United States, 7."

### 3.3 Colons

- Introduces element or series illustrating/amplifying preceding clause. "The watch came with a choice of three bands: stainless steel, plastic, or leather."
- Between independent clauses: colon puts emphasis on what follows (like semicolon but stronger).
- One space after colon in typeset matter (not two).
- Capitalize first word after colon if it introduces a complete sentence (new to 18th ed.).
- Colon after "as follows" / "the following": required. "The steps are as follows: First, make grooves for the seeds; second..."
- DO NOT use colon before a series that serves as object of a verb. "The menagerie included cats, pigeons, newts, and deer ticks." (NOT "included: cats...")
- Colon to introduce speech in dialogue or a quotation (when introduction is a complete sentence).
- Colon in formal correspondence after greeting. "Dear Credit and Collections Manager:"

### 3.4 Dashes (Em dash, En dash, Hyphen)

**Hyphen (-)**
- Joins compound modifiers before a noun: "well-known author"
- Separates numbers that are not inclusive (telephone numbers, ISBNs). "1-800-621-2376"
- Spells out words letter by letter: "p-h-y-l-l-i-s"
- Joins single word/prefix to hyphenated compound: "non-English-speaking peoples"
- See CMOS chapter 7 for full hyphenation rules.

**En Dash (–)**
- Principal use: connecting numbers meaning "up to and including" (through). "1993–2000" | "pages 75–110"
- Do NOT use en dash after "from" (use "from...to"): "from 2019 to 2022" (not "from 2019–22")
- Do NOT use en dash after "between" (use "between...and"): "between 11:30 a.m. and 1:30 p.m."
- Scores and directions: "Green Bay defeated Chicago, 13–6" | "the London–Paris train"
- Unfinished range: "Jack Stag (1950–)"
- Between personal names as compound modifier: "Ali–Frazier match" | "Epstein–Barr virus"
- Compound adjective when one element is an open compound: "post–World War II years"
- British usage: spaced en dash instead of em dash in running text.
- No space on either side when used as "to" in ranges.

**Em Dash (—)**
- Sets off amplifying/explanatory element — alternative to parentheses, commas, or colon. "It was a revival of the most potent image in modern democracy—the revolutionary idea."
- Indicates sudden break or interruption in dialogue. "'Will he—can he—obtain the necessary signatures?'"
- Never use more than two em dashes in a sentence (in non-dialogue).
- No spaces on either side of em dash.
- Question mark or exclamation point may precede em dash; comma, colon, semicolon never do.
- Can set off introductory noun from pronoun: "Consensus—that was the will-o'-the-wisp he doggedly pursued."
- In lieu of quotation marks for dialogue (some European style): each speech new paragraph, no space after dash.

**2-Em and 3-Em Dashes**
- 2-em dash (——): represents missing word or part of a word. Space on both sides if whole word missing; no space if part of word.
- 3-em dash (———): in bibliographies, represents same author as preceding entry. CMOS now recommends repeating the name instead.

### 3.5 Quotation Marks

- Periods and commas ALWAYS precede closing quotation marks (US style). "He described it as a 'short, sharp shock.'"
- Colons and semicolons ALWAYS follow closing quotation marks.
- Question marks and exclamation points: inside if part of quoted matter, outside if part of surrounding sentence.
- Use directional ("smart") quotation marks: " " and ' ' — never the default straight marks.
- Single quotation marks for quotes within quotes.
- Space between nested single and double quotation marks aids legibility.
- Quotation marks in same font style as surrounding text (not necessarily matching enclosed material).
- Block quotations: not enclosed in quotation marks (use indentation or other formatting).
- Words used as words: italicized, not quoted (CMOS preference).

### 3.6 Apostrophes

- Possessive singular: add 's (even for names ending in s — CMOS prefers 's). "the boss's desk" | "Charles's book"
- Possessive plural: add s' (if plural ends in s). "the Smiths' house" | "the dogs' leashes"
- Joint possession: last noun takes possessive. "John and Mary's house"
- Individual possession: each noun takes possessive. "John's and Mary's books"
- Contractions: apostrophe marks omitted letters. "don't" | "it's" (it is) | "rock 'n' roll"
- Plurals of letters: "p's and q's" (apostrophe to avoid confusion).
- Plurals of numbers: no apostrophe. "the 1990s" | "the 1940s" (NOT "1990's" for decades)
- Abbreviated year: apostrophe replaces first two digits. "the class of '06" (opening single quote is WRONG — use apostrophe: '06, not 06)
- Smart apostrophe: same character as right single quotation mark (U+2019). Never use left single quotation mark as apostrophe.
- Apostrophe at end of word: period/comma follows the word, not before the apostrophe. "the Smiths'."

### 3.7 Parentheses and Brackets

**Parentheses**
- Set off material from surrounding text (stronger than comma, like dash). "He suspected that the noble gases (helium, neon, etc.) could produce a similar effect."
- Parentheses within parentheses: use square brackets inside parentheses, not nested parentheses. "(For further discussion see Richardson's analysis [1999].)"
- Opening parenthesis: preceded by comma/semicolon only in enumerations.
- Closing parenthesis: never preceded by comma, semicolon, or colon.
- Period inside if entire sentence is in parentheses; otherwise period outside.
- Question mark/exclamation point: inside if belongs to parenthetical matter; outside if belongs to surrounding sentence.
- Glosses/translations: enclose in parentheses. "mil millones (billion)"

**Square Brackets**
- Enclose editorial interpolations in quoted matter. "'They [the free-silver Democrats] asserted that the ratio could be maintained.'"
- "[sic]" for error in original: "recieved [sic] with thanks"
- Brackets within parentheses (Chicago preference): "(see Richardson [1999])"
- Phonetic transcriptions: enclosed in square brackets.
- Angle brackets < >: for XML/HTML tags, URL wrappers (unnecessary in regular prose).
- Braces { }: for specialized mathematical/programming use; not interchangeable with parentheses.

### 3.8 Ellipses

- Three spaced periods (periods) for omitted material in quotations. (CMOS prefers spaces between dots.)
- " . . . " (space before first dot, between each dot, and after last dot). NOT "..."
- Also used for faltering speech or incomplete thought.
- When omission follows a complete sentence, include the sentence-ending period before the ellipsis (four dots total: period + space + three dots).
- Do NOT combine ellipsis with "etc." or "and so forth" — use one or the other.

### 3.9 Other Punctuation

**Periods**
- End declarative/imperative sentences with a period. "The two faced each other in silence." / "Wait here."
- One space after a period between sentences (not two).
- No period after display lines (headings, captions, etc.) unless ending in an abbreviation.
- Abbreviation-ending period at sentence end: no second period. "Mulvehill & Co." (not "Co..")
- Entire sentence in parentheses: period inside. Fragment within sentence: period outside.

**Slashes**
- Signifies alternatives: "and/or" | "Hercules/Heracles"
- In two-year spans: "1966/67" (alternative to en dash)
- As fraction bar: "1/2" in running text
- In poetry line breaks quoted in text: space before and after slash.
- As "per": "110 km/s" | "$450/week"
- In abbreviations: "c/o" | "n/a"
- In URLs: no spaces. Break before slash at line end.
- Space before/after slash when at least one term is an open compound: "World War I / First World War"

**Multiple Punctuation Marks**
- Abbreviation-ending period at sentence end: no additional period. "Mulvehill & Co."
- Abbreviation period + other punctuation: both appear. "Johnson et al., in How to Survive, describe..."
- Question mark/exclamation point: never accompanied by period (stronger, takes precedence).
- Comma omitted after question mark/exclamation point in quoted speech. "'Are you a doctor?' asked Mahmoud."
- Comma may be retained after title that ends in ? or !: "'Are You a Doctor?,' the fifth story..."
- Double punctuation (?! or !?): avoid in formal prose.

**Spaces**
- One space between sentences (not two).
- One space after colon.
- Nonbreaking space: prevents unwanted line breaks (between numeral and unit, initials in names, etc.).
- Spaces in mathematical expressions: before and after binary operators (+, =, etc.).
- No space between numeral and % symbol: "20%"

**Lists**
- Run-in lists: items in parentheses, separated by commas (or semicolons if items have internal commas).
- Vertical lists introduced by complete sentence + colon.
- Unordered (bulleted) lists: items lowercase unless complete sentences; no end punctuation for fragments.
- Ordered (numbered) lists: period after numeral; capitalize items; punctuation only if complete sentences.
- Vertical lists completing introductory sentence: semicolons between items, period after final item, lowercase start, optional "and" before final item.
- Multilevel outlines: I. > A. > 1. > a) > (1) > (a) > i) — items at each level parallel.

## 4. Numbers (from CMOS Ch.9)

### 4.1 Numerals vs. Words

**General Rule (Chicago default)**
- Spell out whole numbers zero through one hundred. "Thirty-two children from eleven families were packed into eight vintage Beetles."
- Use numerals for numbers above one hundred. "My house is 103 years old." | "The population now stands at 5,893."
- Round multiples (hundred, thousand, hundred thousand) following one through one hundred are also spelled out. "seven hundred spectators" | "two thousand in Lyon" | "more than two hundred thousand"
- Exact vs. approximate: even large round numbers may be spelled out; use numerals for exact figures alongside round ones. "some forty-seven thousand persons" vs. "attendance was 47,122"

**Alternative Rule (zero through nine)**
- Spell out single-digit numbers (zero through nine).
- Use numerals for all others. "13 to 18" | "two were 11 and one was 9"
- Round multiples of hundreds/thousands/hundred thousands: expressed as numerals under alternative rule.

**Ordinals**
- Same rules as cardinal numbers. "the forty-fifth floor" | "125th position"
- Ordinal letters: "122nd" and "123rd" (not "122d" or "123d").
- Do NOT use superscript for ordinal endings. "122nd" not "122^(nd)"
- "Zeroth" (not "zeroth").

**Consistency**
- Maintain consistency within a paragraph or series.
- If one number in a category must be a numeral, use numerals for all in that category.
- Exception allowed at beginning of a sentence.

**Number Beginning a Sentence**
- Always spell out (or reword to avoid). "One hundred ten candidates were accepted." | Better: "In all, 110 candidates were accepted."
- Years may begin a sentence as numerals (new to 18th ed.), but rewording is preferred. "1937 was marked by..." or "The year 1937..."
- Terms combining letters and numerals may begin a sentence: "3D imagery has come a long way..."

**When Numerals Are Always Used**
- With abbreviated units of measure: "3 lb." | "10 km" | "72 dpi"
- Before "percent": "3 percent" | "a 3 percent raise"
- Document parts: "page 2" | "chapter 1" | "vol. 3"
- With symbols: "$3" | "8%" | "5 degrees"
- Streets, rooms: "1427 E. 60th St." | "room 9" | "suite 7A"
- Dates: "June 1, 1941" | "May 5"
- Grades/levels: "grade 3" (but "third grade") | "level 6"
- Scores/vote tallies: "a 34–3 victory" | "a vote of 6 to 3"
- Clothing sizes: "size 12 dress" | "32-inch waist"
- Degree/severity: "type 1 diabetes" | "a Category 5 hurricane"

**Large Numbers**
- Millions/billions: spelled out or numerals per general rule. "three million" | "220 million inhabitants"
- Fractional quantities in millions: mixture of numerals and spelled-out. "2.3 million" | "13.5 billion years"
- Billions: US = thousand million (9 zeros). UK traditionally = million million (12 zeros). Clarify if ambiguous.
- "Mega-", "giga-", "tera-" prefixes: SI system for very large quantities.
- Scientific notation: "5.34 x 10^8 = 534,000,000"
- K = thousand: "priced at $350K"

### 4.2 Dates and Times

**Dates**
- Years: always numerals. "1776" | "3000 BCE"
- Year beginning a sentence: numeral allowed (but reword preferred).
- Abbreviated year: apostrophe replaces first two digits. "the class of '06" (not "06")
- Month and day: cardinal numbers (pronounced as ordinals). "October 4, 2022" (not "October 4th, 2022")
- Day without month: ordinal. "On November 5... By the 25th..."
- All-numeral dates (5/10/99): avoid in formal writing (ambiguous month/day).
- ISO style: "2025-07-14" (year-month-day, hyphenated).
- Decades: numerals or words, no apostrophe before s. "the 1940s" | "the forties and fifties" (NOT "1940's")
- Centuries: spelled out as ordinals, lowercased. "the twenty-first century" | "the eighth and ninth centuries"
- Eras: "21 BCE–39 CE" or "55 BC–AD 1066" (AD/AH precede year; BCE/BC/CE follow).

**Times**
- Even/half/quarter hours: spell out in text. "five o'clock" | "half past three"
- With a.m./p.m.: use numerals. "5:22 a.m." | "11:00 p.m."
- Chicago prefers lowercase a.m./p.m. with periods.
- Do NOT use a.m./p.m. with "morning", "afternoon", "o'clock".
- Noon and midnight: spell out (never "12:00 p.m." or "12:00 a.m."). "by noon" | "midnight, August 21–22"
- 24-hour system: four digits, colon optional. "14:38" | "1500 hours"

### 4.3 Ranges and Inclusive Numbers

- En dash between numbers means "up to and including": "pages 75–110" | "2000–2009"
- With "from": use "from...to" (not "from...–"). "from 75 to 110" (not "from 75–110")
- With "between": use "between...and". "between 150 and 200"
- Abbreviating inclusive numbers (Chicago system):
  - Both under 100: use all digits. "3–10" | "71–72" | "96–117"
  - 100 or multiples of 100: use all digits. "100–104" | "1100–1113"
  - 101–109 etc.: use changed part only. "101–8" | "808–33" | "1103–4"
  - 110–199 etc.: use two digits unless more needed. "321–28" | "498–532" | "1496–500"
- Inclusive years: abbreviate per same pattern (but century change = all digits). "1914–18" | "2024–25" | "1897–1901"
- Inclusive Roman numerals: always give in full. "xxv–xxviii"
- Spelled-out inclusive numbers: use "to" (not en dash). "forty-five to forty-nine years"

### 4.4 Fractions and Decimals

- Simple fractions: spell out, hyphenated in noun/adjective/adverb form. "three-fourths of the book" | "a two-thirds majority"
- Whole number + fraction: spell out if short, else numerals. "three and one-quarter miles" | "6 1/2 yards"
- Mathematical fractions: slash between numerator and denominator. "1/2" | "2/3" | "a/b"
- Decimal fractions for large/complex fractions: "0.73" | "3.14159"
- Percentages: use numerals; "percent" in nontechnical, "%" in scientific. "Less than 3 percent" | "Only 20%"
- At sentence start: spell out. "Thirty-nine percent identified..."
- Decimal fractions: express as numerals. "a mean of 0.73"
- Zero before decimal point for values less than 1.00 (except probabilities, batting averages, firearm calibers). "0.73" | "p < .05" | ".38 special"
- No space between numeral and % symbol: "20%"
- "Percent" (adverb) is NOT interchangeable with "percentage" (noun): "1 percent is a very small percentage"

### 4.5 Money and Currency

- Whole numbers of 100 or less: spell out. "seventy-five cents" | "fifteen dollars"
- Numerals for larger amounts: "$1,000" | "$95,000"
- Millions+: mixture of numerals and spelled-out. "$250 million" | "$7.3 billion"
- Zeros and decimal point: include only if fractional amounts appear in same context. "$0.95" to "$10.00"
- Non-US dollars: identify with prefix. "C$300" or "Can$300" | "NZ$749"
- British currency: "15 pounds" = "15" | "50 pence" = "50p"
- Euros: "40 euros" = "40" or "EUR 40"

### 4.6 Additional Number Conventions

**Addresses**
- Numbered highways: Arabic numerals. "US Route 41" | "Interstate 90"
- Numbered streets: spell out if 100 or less. "First Avenue" | "Ninety-Fifth Street" | "122nd Street"
- Building numbers: Arabic numerals before street name. "1155 South Euclid Avenue"
- Suites/rooms: Arabic numerals. "room 317" | "suite 7A"

**Numbers with Proper Names**
- Monarchs/popes: Roman numerals. "Elizabeth II" | "Innocent III"
- Personal names: Roman or Arabic ordinal — no preceding punctuation. "Adlai E. Stevenson III" | "Michael F. Johnson 2nd"
- Sequels: follow source usage. "The Godfather, Part II" | "Jaws 2"
- Vessels: Roman numerals. "Bluebird III"
- Spacecraft: Arabic numerals. "Voyager 2"
- Successive governments (dynasties, congresses): ordinals, spell out if 100 or less. "Eighteenth Dynasty" | "Fifth Republic" | "117th Congress"

**Telephone Numbers**
- US: (XXX) XXX-XXXX or XXX-XXX-XXXX (hyphens, not periods).
- International: + symbol before country code, spaces as separators. "+52 55 xxxx xxxx"
- Extension: separated by comma. "ext. XXX"

**Ratios**
- Spell out with "to" in ordinary text. "a three-to-one ratio"
- Numerals with colon: no spaces on either side of colon. "a 13:2 ratio"

**Roman Numerals**
- Smaller letter before larger = subtract; smaller after larger = add.
- Bar over letter = multiply by 1,000.
- Common values: I=1, V=5, X=10, L=50, C=100, D=500, M=1,000.
- Chicago prefers Arabic numerals in most contexts (volume numbers, chapter numbers).
- Roman numerals retained for: front matter pagination, monarchs, personal names, sequels, vessels, outline levels.

**Plurals and Punctuation of Numbers**
- Plurals of spelled-out numbers: normal noun plurals. "twenties and thirties" | "sixes and sevens"
- Plurals of numerals: add "s" — NO apostrophe. "240s" | "238s" | "the 1920s" (NOT "1920's")
- Commas in numerals of 1,000+: between groups of three digits from right. "1,512" | "32,987" | "4,000,500"
- No comma: page numbers, line numbers, addresses, years (years of 5+ digits get comma). "page 1535" | "11030 South Langley" | "35,000 BP"
- Scientific writing: four-digit numbers often omit comma.
- Decimal marker: period in US; comma in many other countries.
- SI style: spaces instead of commas between digit groups. "33 333,33"

## Proofreading Principles

1. Only mark actual errors. Do not over-correct.
2. Consider context before flagging — some constructions are correct in context.
3. `[#NNNN]` is a positional identifier — do NOT modify or proofread it.
4. Each error_id must correspond to an actual `[#NNNN]` marker in the text.
5. Annotation scope must be limited to the erroneous word/phrase only.
6. Only return entries with actual errors. Do NOT return confirmations of correct text.
7. Line breaks in the text represent PDF structural separations, not sentence boundaries. Do not flag punctuation errors due to line breaks.
